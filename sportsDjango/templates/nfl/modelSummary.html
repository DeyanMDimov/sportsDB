Summary for Week {{weekOfSeason}} in {{yearOfSeason}}

{% if selectedModel == "v1" %}
  1. Average the team's offensive yards per game with opponents defensive yards allowed per game.
  2. Average the team's offensive yards per point with the opponents defensive yards per point allowed.
  3. Divide the result from Step 1 (expected offensive yards in the game) by the result from Step 2 (expected yards per point).
  4. Result from Step 3 is your expected score.
  5. If it's game 1 of season, look at average of all regular season games from previous year.
  6. If it's not game 1 of season, take the average of all the games that happened this season.
{% elif selectedModel == "v1.5" %}
  1. Take the average number for the following stats, from the last x games. Length determines the number of games.
  2. This will look at last season's results as well if it's early in the season.
  3. Average the team's offensive yards per game with opponents defensive yards allowed per game.
  4. Average the team's offensive yards per point with the opponents defensive yards per point allowed.
  5. Divide the result from Step 1 (expected offensive yards in the game) by the result from Step 2 (expected yards per point).
  6. Result from Step 3 is your expected score.
{% elif selectedModel == "v3" %}
  1. Every team gets an offense rating (points it scores above an average team) and a defense rating (points it holds an average opponent below their usual score). Zero is league average.
  2. All the ratings are solved for together from this season's and last season's results, so points scored against a good defense count for more than the same points against a bad one.
  3. Recent games count more: a game 10 weeks old counts half as much as last week's, and the offseason ages last year's games by another 8 weeks.
  4. Every rating is pulled toward average, as if the team had also played 4 games at exactly league average. That keeps a couple of unusual games from producing an extreme rating.
  5. Expected score = league average score, plus the team's offense rating, minus the opponent's defense rating, plus half of home field for the home team and minus half for the visitor.
  6. Each team's score then moves by its quarterback adjustment: how many points better or worse this week's starter has passed than the quarterbacks who played the games behind the team's rating. It is zero when the same quarterback played all of them.
  7. For a game that hasn't been played, the model assumes the quarterback who started the team's last game, unless he is listed as out. He is marked "expected". If someone else will start, pick him under "Change Starter" in the game's details and the numbers are redone with him.
  8. The model only names a bet when its line or total is at least 1 point away from the book's. "Edge" is the size of that gap.

Backtested from 2019 on, this lands about 10.1 points from the final margin in a typical game, against 9.8 for the closing line, and its bets against the closing line have won about half the time. docs/betting-model-v3.md has the full explanation.
